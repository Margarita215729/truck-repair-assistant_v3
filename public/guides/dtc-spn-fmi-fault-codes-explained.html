Fault codes

What do DTC, SPN & FMI codes actually mean?

A DTC is the general term for a fault code. Most heavy-duty trucks report codes in the J1939 format instead: an SPN (which part or system triggered it) paired with an FMI (what kind of failure it is — too high, too low, out of range, etc.). Neither number alone tells you the urgency — that depends on the warning color and what the truck is doing.

What this usually means

The SPN identifies the system or component the fault relates to (aftertreatment, engine, electrical, and so on). The FMI describes the nature of the fault the truck's computer detected — a signal reading too high, too low, missing entirely, or out of the expected range. Two trucks throwing the same SPN with different FMI values can point to very different underlying causes, which is why the pair matters more than either number alone.

Manufacturers also layer their own proprietary codes on top of the standard ones, so the exact same symptom can display differently across a Freightliner, a Kenworth, and a Volvo. That's normal — it's part of why reading the code is only the first step, not the answer by itself.

What to do right now

The warning color matters more than the code number

A steady amber/yellow lamp with no other symptoms is usually something to get checked soon, not an emergency. A red warning lamp, a flashing lamp, a derate, or any noticeable change in how the truck handles means: get off the road safely and stop.

This is general guidance, not a diagnosis of your specific truck. Always verify anything safety-critical with a qualified technician.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between a DTC and an SPN/FMI code?
DTC (Diagnostic Trouble Code) is the general term from passenger-car OBD-II systems, usually a letter and four digits. Most Class 7/8 trucks instead use the heavy-duty J1939 standard, which pairs an SPN (Suspect Parameter Number — which component or system) with an FMI (Failure Mode Identifier — what kind of failure). You may see both terms used loosely to mean the same thing: the code your truck is reporting.
Can I keep driving with a check engine light on?
It depends on what else is happening, not the light alone. A steady amber warning with no other symptoms is often manageable short-term but should be checked soon. A flashing or red warning, a derate, or any change in how the truck handles means you should get off the road safely and stop. When in doubt, treat it as something to address before continuing, not after.
Do I need a scan tool to find my code?
Not always. Many trucks display an active code directly on the dash cluster or through the odometer/menu button sequence. A scan tool gives you more detail and history, but you often don't need one just to read what's currently active.
